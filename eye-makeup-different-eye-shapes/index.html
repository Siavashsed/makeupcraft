Eye Makeup for Different Eye Shapes: A Real Breakdown From a Working MUA

By Sofia Marino

Photo by Artem Podrez / Pexels

Here's something that frustrates me every time I see it: a tutorial that tells someone with hooded eyes to apply a dramatic cut crease, then wonder why it disappears the second they open their eyes. Eye shape is probably the single most misunderstood variable in makeup education. According to a 2021 survey by the Professional Beauty Association, nearly 68% of makeup consumers reported feeling like standard beauty tutorials didn't apply to their specific features. That number doesn't surprise me at all. Most tutorials are written for one idealized eye shape, and everyone else is left guessing.

My position is straightforward: the so-called universal rules of eye makeup don't exist. What exists are principles, and those principles behave differently depending on the structure of the eye you're working on. I've spent over twelve years doing editorial, bridal, and film work, and the single biggest shift in my teaching has been moving clients and students away from copying looks and toward understanding their own anatomy. Once you know what you're working with, the technique choices become obvious. So let me walk through the four shapes I work with most, and what I'd actually do on each one.

Hooded Eyes: Stop Fighting the Fold, Work With It

Hooded eyes have a layer of skin that droops over the crease when the eye is open. The fold essentially hides whatever you place directly in the crease, which is why the classic crease blending technique falls flat. The fix isn't to avoid shadow altogether. It's to place your transition color higher than feels comfortable, above where you think the crease is, roughly 4 to 6 millimeters above the natural socket line depending on the degree of hooding.

Liner placement matters enormously here. A thick line along the upper lash line will make the eye feel heavier and smaller. I'd recommend a very thin liner application, almost a lash tightline rather than a traditional wing. If a wing is what you're after, draw it with the eye open and angle it upward at a steeper degree than you'd use on a non-hooded eye. The wing needs to peek out from under the fold to be visible, so it has to travel upward, not outward.

One thing that genuinely doesn't always work on hooded eyes is shimmer all over the lid. I've seen this advice given constantly, the idea that shimmer opens the eye. But if the lid space is largely hidden when the eye is open, shimmer just adds texture to something no one can see. A precise shimmer placement in the very center of the lid, combined with matte transition shades elsewhere, gives the dimensional effect without wasting product on invisible real estate.

Monolid Eyes: Rewriting the Crease Rule Entirely

Monolids don't have a visible crease. That's not a flaw to correct; it's a different canvas. And yet, most Western makeup education still treats monolids as a hooded-eye problem, which they're not. On a monolid, you have a smooth, continuous lid surface from lash line to brow bone. That means you can build graphic shapes and color blocking in ways that are genuinely difficult to achieve on other eye shapes.

When I worked on a project with a Korean beauty brand in 2019, I spent three weeks focused entirely on monolid techniques, and it completely changed how I approach shadow placement for everyone. The key insight was that depth doesn't need to be placed in a crease to read as depth. On monolids, you build dimension by layering color intensity from the lash line upward, darker at the root, graduating to lighter tones higher on the lid. Done with a small, dense brush, this creates the illusion of dimension without mimicking a crease that isn't there.

Liner is where monolid makeup really comes alive. Because there's no fold to obscure it, a bold liner look will stay visible when the eye opens. Graphic liner shapes, floating liner, and two-tone liner all work particularly well. I'd argue that monolids are arguably the most technically exciting shape to work with once you stop trying to impose crease-based techniques onto them.

Downturned and Upturned Eyes: It's All About the Outer Corner

These two shapes are essentially mirror problems, and understanding one helps you understand the other. Downturned eyes have an outer corner that sits lower than the inner corner, giving a soft, slightly sleepy quality to the eye. The common advice is to wing liner upward at the outer corner to counteract the downward angle. That's correct, but the execution is where most people go wrong. The wing shouldn't start at the lash line and travel up at a sharp angle. It should follow the lower lash line outward and then flick up, creating a gradual lift rather than a jarring angle change.

For shadow on downturned eyes, placing the darkest color at the outer corner and blending it upward and outward works well. But keep that color above the lower lash line on the outer third. Smudging dark shadow along the entire lower lash line reinforces the downward direction of the eye. On a downturned eye, I'd use lower lash liner only on the inner half, and keep the outer lower lash line clean or very softly blended.

Upturned eyes have the opposite dynamic: the outer corner sits higher, giving an almond-like lift. The main consideration is not to exaggerate the upward angle further with a steep wing. A flatter, more horizontal liner application balances the shape. And placing a slightly deeper shadow at the inner corner rather than only at the outer corner adds balance. So the technique logic inverts almost completely between these two shapes, which is exactly why one-size advice is useless.

Close-Set and Wide-Set Eyes: Spacing Is a Shadow Problem

The distance between the eyes relative to the width of one eye is what defines close-set versus wide-set. On a balanced face, the space between the eyes roughly equals the width of one eye. Close-set eyes sit nearer to the nose; wide-set eyes have more space between them. Both are extremely common, and both respond dramatically to where you place light and dark tones.

For close-set eyes, the goal is to visually push the eyes apart. That means keeping the inner corner light, even shimmery, while placing depth on the outer half of the lid and outer corner. Avoid dark shadow at the inner corner and avoid liner that begins right at the inner corner tear duct. Starting liner about a third of the way in from the inner corner immediately creates a sense of space. In my experience, this adjustment alone, without changing anything else, makes a visible difference in under 90 seconds of application time.

Wide-set eyes work in the opposite direction. You want to bring visual weight toward the center. That means using a medium-depth shade at the inner corner, bringing liner all the way to the inner corner, and keeping the outer corner treatment lighter or more neutral. A common misconception here is that wide-set eyes always need heavy inner corner lining to look balanced. Actually, overloading the inner corner creates a heavy, strange effect. The goal is a gentle gradient inward, not a dramatic dark block. Subtlety wins on wide-set eyes far more often than people expect.

The one thing I'd ask you to do today is look at your own eyes before you open any tutorial. Identify your shape, even roughly, and then ask whether the technique you're about to follow was actually designed for that structure. If the answer is no, don't abandon the look. Instead, adjust the placement to suit your anatomy. Move the shadow up, shift the liner start point, change the blending direction. The look can still be achieved; it just needs to be translated. That translation skill is what separates a makeup artist who knows how to apply product from one who knows how to make it work.
