Fenty Beauty's 40-Shade Foundation Launched in 2017, But 2019 Was When the Industry Finally Had to Respond

What the Data Actually Said About Shade Gaps in 2019

The stats that circulated in early 2019 were startling even to people already paying attention. A Refinery29 analysis found that the average foundation line still offered significantly fewer shades for deeper skin tones than for light to medium ranges, with the deepest 20% of the skin tone spectrum covered by a fraction of the available options. That wasn't a fringe finding. It matched what working MUAs had been saying for years.

And then there's the purchasing side. NPD Group reported that prestige beauty sales grew around 6% in 2019, with foundation and complexion products driving a significant portion of that growth. Brands that had expanded shade ranges post-Fenty were capturing spend that had previously gone elsewhere, often to specialty retailers or independent brands that had been serving deeper skin tones all along. The money was always there. The product wasn't.

If you're thinking about how shade gaps connect to formula and undertone, this is exactly the kind of problem I cover in my foundation matching and undertone guide. Understanding undertone isn't a luxury skill for high-end work. It's baseline competence, and the 2019 conversation made that impossible to ignore at every tier of the market.

How Legacy Brands Responded and Why Some of It Missed the Mark

The response from established brands in 2019 was real but uneven. Several major cosmetics houses added shade extensions to existing lines, some going from 20 shades to 40 or more within a single relaunch cycle. On paper, that sounds like progress. In practice, I tested a number of these extensions on clients through the spring and summer of 2019, and a consistent problem kept showing up: the deeper shades were frequently ashy, pulling grey or purple on medium-deep and deep skin tones because the undertone calibration hadn't kept pace with the shade expansion.

This is the part that doesn't get talked about enough. Adding shade numbers doesn't mean adding shade quality. A line with 50 shades that handles deep skin tones poorly is not better than a focused line with 30 well-formulated options. The brands that got it right in 2019 were the ones that hired colorists and consultants with actual expertise in deep skin pigmentation, not the ones that ran a linear interpolation from their existing deepest shade and called it a day.

There's a genuine debate in the industry about whether shade count alone is a meaningful metric, and I'm on the side that says it absolutely isn't. What matters is distribution across the full spectrum, undertone accuracy at every depth, and formula performance on a range of skin types. Those are harder to market on a product card, but they're what your clients will feel in six hours when their foundation starts to look chalky.

What This Meant for Working Makeup Artists on the Ground

For those of us doing editorial, bridal, or commercial work in 2019, the shift was genuinely practical. Client expectations changed. Deep-toned clients started arriving at consultations with screenshots of specific shades they'd seen reviewed online, with language about undertones they'd learned from YouTube tutorials, asking pointed questions. That's a good thing. It raised the bar for preparation.

I've seen this pattern play out across years of client work: when consumers get more sophisticated, the MUAs who haven't kept up get left behind fast. The 2019 moment accelerated that. If you were still working from a kit built around five or six foundation shades and mixing your way through the rest, that approach became visibly inadequate. Not because mixing is wrong, but because clients could now walk into a store and find something close to their shade off the shelf. Your job became about precision and fit, not just approximation.

For eye work specifically, the conversation about inclusive techniques was also building. If you're adapting technique to a full client range, my breakdown of eye makeup for different eye shapes is worth having in your back pocket, because the client diversity driving the shade range conversation was the same diversity demanding technique fluency across eye morphology.

The Longer Signal Behind the 2019 Noise

Okay, real talk for a second. My friend Dara, who is a beauty buyer at a mid-size retailer, told me sometime in early 2019 that her team was under explicit instruction to audit every new foundation pitch for shade range before it got to the buying committee. She said it in a kind of exhausted, 'finally but also why did it take this long' tone that I think captures the industry mood perfectly. I'm not 100% sure this kind of formal gatekeeping was universal, but anecdotally, the shift in buyer behavior was real and it showed up in what actually made it onto shelves.

The broader signal in 2019 was about accountability becoming structural rather than anecdotal. It wasn't just beauty editors writing opinion pieces anymore. Brands were being called out by name in viral social posts, and that reputational pressure was translating into board-level decisions about product development. That's a different kind of pressure than trend coverage. It's the kind that changes hiring, budget allocation, and formulation timelines.

Whether all of that momentum has been sustained is a fair question for 2020 and beyond. But in 2019, the industry was in a live, visible reckoning, and the MUAs paying attention were the ones who came out of it better positioned. The brands that treated it as a PR cycle to manage rather than a product quality problem to solve... those are the ones whose shade extensions ended up in the discount bin by Q4.

If you take one thing from the 2019 shade range moment, let it be this: audit your kit right now for undertone depth, not just shade depth. Pull out your darkest five foundations and check whether they have genuine warm, neutral, and cool options within that range. If they don't, that's the gap your clients with deep and deep-cool skin tones are living with every time they sit in your chair. Fix that gap first, and the rest of the inclusivity conversation becomes technique rather than theory.
