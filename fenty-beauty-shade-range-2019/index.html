Fenty Beauty's 50-Shade Promise Changed Foundation Matching in 2019 and We're Still Feeling It

Sofia Marino on how Fenty Beauty's expanded shade range in 2019 reshaped how MUAs approach foundation matching and client consultations.

The moment a brand makes 50 shades standard, every other foundation line suddenly looks like a failure of imagination. That's exactly where we landed in 2019, as Fenty Beauty's influence from its 2017 launch had fully rippled through the industry and competitors were scrambling to catch up with their own extended ranges. I remember sitting backstage at a bridal show in Milan in early 2019 with a kit that, for the first time, actually matched every single one of the twelve models in front of me. I'd spent years travelling with over 80 individual foundations from five different brands just to manage that kind of range. That morning I had two palettes and a handful of drops. It was the first time I didn't feel like I was apologising to someone's skin.

What made 2019 significant wasn't just the product itself. It was the cultural and professional reckoning that followed. Brands like L'Oréal, Maybelline, and MAC all expanded their foundation ranges in direct response to what Fenty had started. But the real story for working MUAs was what this meant for technique. More shades meant more nuance required. You couldn't just grab the closest neutral anymore. You had to actually understand undertones, depth, and how light interacts with skin across the full range of human complexions. The industry was, arguably, more technically demanding than it had been in a decade.

What Fenty's Influence Actually Looked Like on the Ground in 2019

By early 2019, Business of Fashion was reporting that Fenty Beauty had earned an estimated $100 million in its first 40 days of sales back in 2017, and two years later that commercial pressure had forced real structural change across the market. We weren't just seeing token additions of a couple of darker shades. We were seeing full re-architecture of shade systems. Brands were retiring old skus and rebuilding from scratch. That's not a small thing. That's a supply chain, a formulation lab, and a marketing department all pivoting at once.

In my work with emerging MUAs during my 2019 workshops, I noticed that younger artists were walking in with different expectations than the generation before them. They assumed diversity in the kit. They weren't grateful for one or two deep shades as a bonus. They treated it as the baseline. That shift in professional expectation is what the Fenty effect actually produced, and it was visible in real time. Training programmes started updating their curriculum. Kit requirements changed. The conversation moved.

According to a McKinsey & Company analysis of beauty market dynamics, inclusive product ranges became one of the top three growth drivers in prestige beauty by 2019, with brands reporting measurable sales gains when they genuinely expanded shade diversity rather than adding a few token options. The numbers backed up what we were seeing on the ground.

Why Undertone Knowledge Became Non-Negotiable for MUAs

More shades didn't automatically mean better matches. And this is where a lot of artists got tripped up. Having 50 shades is only useful if you know how to read skin accurately. Undertones, specifically the difference between warm, cool, and neutral, had always mattered. But with a narrow shade range, a slightly wrong undertone got hidden by the limited options available. When you've got 50 choices, the wrong undertone becomes obvious instantly. It sits on the skin like a mask instead of disappearing into it.

If you want a solid technical foundation for this, I'd point you toward a proper guide on foundation matching by undertone, because understanding that system is what separates a mediocre application from one that actually works. In 2019, that knowledge went from being a nice-to-have to something clients actively expected because they'd seen what a correct match looked like on social media.

The misconception I kept correcting in workshops was that 'more shades equals easier matching.' It's actually the opposite. Precision becomes harder when the range is wider because the margin for error shrinks. You have to commit to your undertone read and trust it. That's a skill. It takes practice and, honestly, a lot of wasted product along the way.

The Industry Response and What It Got Wrong

Here's where I have to be honest about what the competitive scramble actually produced. Not every brand that rushed to expand its range did it well. Some added shades without adjusting the formula, which meant the new deeper options oxidised differently, separated faster, or had undertone inconsistencies that weren't present in the original lineup. I tested at least six 'extended range' launches in 2019, and three of them had obvious formulation issues in the deeper shades specifically. That's a 50% miss rate, which is bad. For more on this, see Fenty Beauty's 40-Shade Foundation Launched in 2017, But 2019 Was When the Industry Finally Had to Respond.

The brands that did it correctly, and there were several, took the time to rebalance pigment ratios across the full depth range. Pigment load in a deep foundation is fundamentally different from a light one. More titanium dioxide shifts undertones. More iron oxides affect oxidation. Matching across that spectrum requires real R&D investment, not just a marketing announcement. Some brands made that investment. Others didn't, and artists and clients both noticed.

Okay, I have to tell you something embarrassing. I confidently recommended one of these 'new extended' ranges to my friend Daniela, who has a deep warm complexion, and she came back to me three days later holding up her arm to show me where the foundation had turned properly orange by noon. I had swatched it on my hand, which is, objectively, not Daniela's arm, and I should have known better. I still hear about it. She calls it 'the tangerine incident.' I'm not 100% sure any swatch on a pale hand tells you what a deep shade will do on actual deep skin, and I think a lot of us were learning that in real time in 2019.

What This Moment Meant for How We Train and What Comes Next

The 2019 foundation landscape shift had a direct impact on how makeup education programmes were structured. Colour theory as applied to diverse skin tones became a required module in serious training, not an elective. Curriculum writers had to account for the full spectrum of human complexion rather than centering pale to medium skin as the default with darker skin as a specialised topic. That structural change in education has lasting effects because it changes what the next generation of MUAs considers normal.

It also opened up a broader conversation about product regulation and ingredient transparency that's still ongoing. When you're formulating for deeper skin tones, pigment chemistry matters more, and some ingredients that behave neutrally on lighter skin have different optical and chemical effects on melanin-rich complexions. That's a technical area where regulatory frameworks, like those discussed in the context of EU cosmetics regulation changes affecting makeup artists, are increasingly relevant because ingredient disclosure directly affects a client's ability to make informed choices about what goes on their skin.

The real legacy of 2019 isn't the products themselves. It's the professional standard they established. A working MUA who can't match across a full complexion range is now visibly undertrained, and that's a direct result of what Fenty started and what 2019 turned into an industry expectation. For artists working with clients across diverse eye shapes, skin tones, and features, that technical range matters across every category, not just foundation. The kind of attentiveness that makes you good at adapting eye makeup for different eye shapes is the same attentiveness that makes you good at reading skin. It's all connected.

If you take one thing away from what 2019 taught us: audit your kit today against the actual diversity of clients you serve. If your foundation range doesn't cover at least 40 shades with genuine undertone variation across depth levels, you're already behind. And if you have the shades but haven't practised reading undertones in depth ranges you're less familiar with, that's your next training priority. Technique without tools is incomplete. Tools without technique are useless. You need both, and 2019 made sure we knew it.
