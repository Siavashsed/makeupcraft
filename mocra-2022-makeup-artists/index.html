MoCRA Just Passed, and Every Makeup Kit Is About to Change

Congress just gave the FDA real power over cosmetics for the first time since 1938. Here is what MoCRA 2022 means for working makeup artists.

Words by Bibiana Russo · July 12, 2022

Congress just handed the FDA its first real authority over cosmetics since 1938, and it slipped into law on December 29, 2022, buried inside a government spending bill almost nobody read. It's called MoCRA, the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act, and if you touch product for a living, it will reach your kit. I found out the way most of us find out anything, scrolling at 11 p.m. after a bridal trial, when a supplier email dropped with the subject line "registration deadlines." I actually thought it was spam. It wasn't.

Here's the short version. For the better part of a century, the agency that regulates the powder on your beauty blender had less enforcement muscle than the one that regulates dog food. No mandatory recalls. No required ingredient safety records. MoCRA changes that, and it does it faster than the industry expected. So let's talk about what actually shifts, what's hype, and what you should do before your favorite foundation gets pulled.

What Actually Changed on December 29

The old rulebook was the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938. Think about that. The last time cosmetics law got a serious update, foundation came in about four shades and lead was a normal ingredient. MoCRA bolts several new powers onto that ancient frame, and the big one is mandatory recall authority. Before this, if a brand shipped a mascara that was giving people chemical burns, the FDA could ask nicely. Now it can order the product off shelves.

The other pillars matter just as much to us. Facilities that manufacture cosmetics have to register with the FDA. Every product needs a responsible person on file who keeps safety substantiation records. And there's an adverse event reporting system, so a serious reaction gets logged instead of vanishing into a customer service inbox. You can read the framework straight from the source at the FDA's MoCRA page. It's dry, but it's the real document.

My take: this is overdue and it's good. I've watched too many indie launches skip stability testing and hit the market on vibes and a nice label. A paper trail forces a floor.

Why the Talc and PFAS Panic Set This in Motion

MoCRA didn't come from nowhere. It came from years of headlines about asbestos-contaminated talc and the slow realization that "long-lasting" often meant PFAS, the forever chemicals, sitting in your setting products. A 2021 study in Environmental Science and Technology Letters tested 231 cosmetics sold in the US and Canada and found high fluorine, a PFAS marker, in 52 percent of them. Foundations and waterproof mascaras were among the worst offenders. That number rattled a lot of us who'd been dusting those exact formulas onto clients' faces for a decade.

MoCRA specifically directs the FDA to assess talc for asbestos and to study PFAS use in cosmetics. That's not a ban. Let me be clear, because the coverage is already sloppy on this point. It's a mandate to look, measure, and report. But a mandate to look is how bans start, and the smart brands know it. The Part Most Artists Are Getting Wrong

So I keep seeing artists panic that MoCRA means they personally have to register with the FDA, and honestly I get why, the language is a mess. But here's the thing. The registration and responsible-person burden falls on manufacturers and the brands that own the products, not on you doing a smoky eye at a hotel suite in Newark. If you're reselling or private-labeling your own line, that's different, and you should talk to someone who knows this cold.

I'll admit I'm not 100 percent certain how the small-business carve-outs shake out yet, and anyone who tells you they are is guessing. My friend runs a tiny two-person lip balm brand out of her garage, and she spent a whole weekend convinced she was about to owe the government a facility registration and a compliance officer she can't afford. Turns out there are exemptions for smaller companies under certain revenue thresholds. She cried, then made me a gin and tonic. The details still aren't fully settled, and that's the honest state of it in late 2022.

What is settled: your job gets easier to defend. When a client with reactive skin asks what's in a product, you'll increasingly have a real answer on file, not a shrug.

How This Fits the Bigger Regulatory Picture

The US is playing catch-up here, and it helps to see it in context. Europe has restricted or banned well over a thousand cosmetic ingredients for years while the US had banned roughly a dozen. That gap is exactly why I've always cross-checked labels against EU standards when I build a sensitive-skin kit, and it's why the coming European rule changes are worth your attention too. I wrote about that in my breakdown of the EU Cosmetics Regulation shake-up and what it means for artists, and MoCRA is the US quietly inching toward that same posture.

You can see the momentum in product design already. The rise of skin-forward tints with actual SPF filed under drugstore prices, like I covered in the piece on why 2022's drugstore SPF-tint moment mattered, wasn't just a trend. It was brands getting ahead of scrutiny by simplifying formulas. And the shade-inclusion reckoning that Fenty forced back in 2019 proved the same lesson MoCRA is now codifying: when the rules or the market demand accountability, the whole category levels up.

Do one thing this week. Pull the three products you use on almost every client, the ride-or-die foundation, the setting powder, the waterproof mascara, and look up who the responsible party is on the packaging or the brand's site. Save it in a note on your phone. When a formula gets reformulated or recalled under these new rules, and some will, you'll know in thirty seconds whether it's in your kit instead of finding out from a client's rash. Frequently asked questions

Does MoCRA mean freelance makeup artists have to register with the FDA?

No. The registration and record-keeping duties fall on the manufacturers and the brands that own the products. If you only apply purchased products on clients, MoCRA does not require you to register. It changes only if you make or private-label your own line.

When did MoCRA actually become law?

It was signed into law on December 29, 2022, tucked inside the Consolidated Appropriations Act. It is the biggest expansion of FDA authority over cosmetics since 1938.

Will MoCRA ban talc or PFAS in makeup?

Not immediately. The law directs the FDA to assess asbestos in talc and to study PFAS use, then report findings. It is a mandate to investigate and measure, which is often the first step toward future restrictions.
