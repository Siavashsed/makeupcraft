Fenty Skin Launched in 2020 and Forced the Beauty Industry to Stop Treating Skincare as an Afterthought

Fenty Skin's 2020 launch reshaped how makeup artists think about skincare prep. Sofia Marino on why it mattered then and still matters now.

Words by Sofia Marino · May 17, 2020

What Made Fenty Skin Different From Every Other Celebrity Skincare Launch

Celebrity skincare lines had a reputation problem going into 2020. Most of them were overpriced, under-formulated, and sold entirely on name recognition rather than ingredient integrity. Fenty Skin came out with a three-step system that was specifically formulated for melanin-rich skin, with SPF 30 in the moisturizer (the Hydra Vizor), and a glycolic-based toner called Fat Water. These weren't filler products. The formulations were created with input from Rihanna herself about what her skin actually needed, and the price points sat at roughly $25 to $35 per product, which was deliberately accessible.

For professional makeup artists, the accessibility piece mattered enormously. I've seen too many skincare lines aimed at 'diverse skin' that cost $150 per serum, which means clients who need those formulations most are priced out entirely. Fenty Skin's price structure meant that artists working with a range of clients, including clients who weren't wealthy, could actually recommend and use these products on set. That's not a small thing.

The gender-neutral branding was also a genuine industry first at this scale. Fenty Skin launched with male-presenting models and an explicit 'for everyone' message that wasn't just marketing language. It changed how some of my male clients talked about skincare prep, which had always been an uphill conversation before. Suddenly they were coming in asking about the Fat Water by name.

The Skincare-First Prep Movement That Fenty Skin Accelerated for MUAs

Here's something that doesn't get said enough in makeup education: the quality of your canvas determines the ceiling of your work. Most makeup training focuses almost entirely on product application technique, color theory, and tool use. Skincare prep is treated as a sidebar. Fenty Skin's 2020 launch became a cultural moment that gave professional artists a public, mainstream entry point for explaining to clients why prep matters as much as the makeup itself. For more on this, see Fenty Beauty's 40-Shade Foundation Launched in 2017, But 2019 Was When the Industry Finally Had to Respond.

According to a NPD Group report from late 2020, prestige skincare sales in the US grew by 11% year-over-year despite the broader beauty market contracting, which was a striking inversion of the usual pattern. Clients were educated. They were reading ingredients. And they were starting to ask their makeup artists real questions about skin barrier function and hydration levels before a shoot or event. That's a shift in the professional relationship, not just a shopping trend.

In my work with students at beauty workshops, I started incorporating a full skincare prep module into my foundation application courses in the second half of 2020, largely because of how many clients were now arriving having already done their own skincare research. You can't teach foundation matching in a vacuum anymore. Which is also why a solid understanding of undertones and skin prep together has become non-negotiable in my curriculum.

The Criticism Was Real and Deserves a Straight Answer

Fenty Skin wasn't universally praised. Some dermatologists flagged the Fat Water toner's glycolic acid concentration as potentially irritating for sensitive or compromised skin barriers, especially given that the pandemic had a lot of people over-exfoliating at home. That's a fair criticism. Glycolic acid at regular use frequency can absolutely cause sensitivity issues if someone's skin is already reactive, and Fenty Skin's marketing didn't always make that caveat prominent enough.

There was also ongoing debate about whether the SPF 30 in Hydra Vizor was sufficient as a standalone sunscreen, particularly for deeper skin tones. The American Academy of Dermatology recommends SPF 30 as a minimum, so it technically met the standard, but some dermatologists argued that SPF 50 should be the baseline recommendation for daily use, especially for melanin-rich skin that has historically been undertreated for skin cancer risk. I'm not 100% sure the SPF conversation has a clean resolution even now, honestly.

Okay, I have to say this somewhere because it's genuinely funny in retrospect: my friend Daniela, who is a brilliant esthetician, tried the whole three-step Fenty Skin system the first week it launched and called me at about 11pm to say her skin felt 'like a glazed donut but in a concerning way.' She'd layered the toner, the serum, and the moisturizer on top of her existing four-step routine without removing anything first. She was using approximately nine products in one sitting. The Total Cleans'r sitting untouched on her bathroom shelf. Sometimes we forget that 'start with step one' is actually the instruction. For more on this, see Fenty Beauty's 50-Shade Promise Changed Foundation Matching in 2019 and We're Still Feeling It.

Why This Moment Reshaped Professional Standards, Not Just Consumer Habits

What Fenty Skin did in 2020 that no other product launch managed was bridge the gap between consumer skincare culture and professional makeup practice in a publicly visible way. Before this, those two worlds talked past each other constantly. Skincare people dismissed makeup as 'covering up' rather than working with skin. Makeup artists often treated skincare as the client's problem to sort out before they arrived. Fenty Skin made both groups look at the same products and have the same conversation at the same time.

For educators like me, that created a rare opening. Clients who'd spent lockdown reading about niacinamide and hyaluronic acid were suddenly receptive to a conversation about why primer choice and hydration levels affect powder adhesion. That's a technical discussion that used to take twenty minutes of convincing. In the second half of 2020, clients were bringing it up themselves. The beauty education landscape, particularly for working artists, shifted because the public's baseline knowledge shifted. That's significant in a way that a standard product launch simply isn't.

It's also worth placing this in the longer arc of inclusive beauty that Fenty had already started. The brand had spent three years building trust with consumers who'd historically been ignored or tokenized by prestige beauty, and that trust transferred directly to Fenty Skin. Regulatory bodies haven't always kept pace with these shifts in formulation standards for diverse skin tones, which is a separate and ongoing concern, but for those watching how cosmetics regulation shapes what artists can and can't use, the Fenty Skin launch also quietly pressured other brands to revisit their ingredient stories for melanin-rich skin.

If you're a working makeup artist right now, take one concrete thing from this moment: build a three-question skincare intake into your client consultation, before you touch a single product. Ask what they're currently using, what their skin feels like at 3pm on an average day, and whether they've introduced anything new in the last four weeks. Those three questions will tell you more about how their skin will behave under your work than any visual assessment alone. Fenty Skin made this kind of conversation normal with clients. Use that opening.
